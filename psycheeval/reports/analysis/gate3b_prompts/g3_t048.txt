Vague negative feedback is frustrating, especially when it lands without examples. But “they’re threatened by me” is an inference, not a fact.

Three layers to separate:

- What was said: vague negative feedback.
- What they likely meant: some concern they either couldn’t or wouldn’t state clearly.
- What you inferred: the motive is threat.

A different reading is more common: not threat, just weak management. Conflict-avoidant managers often give blurry criticism because they don’t want the discomfort of being specific. Another possible reading is that they’re passing along a broader concern badly. With the evidence you gave, that is more probable than “they’re threatened,” because vagueness is common; threat is possible but needs more proof.

I would not go over their head first. That move can work, but it usually burns political capital unless you can show a pattern and a failed attempt to resolve it directly.

A better sequence:

1. Ask for specifics in writing or in a 1:1.
2. Listen for whether they can name behaviors, examples, and impact.
3. Document the conversation.
4. Escalate only if they stay vague, retaliate, or the feedback affects pay, scope, or performance review.

Use something like this with your boss:

“Thanks for the feedback. I want to act on it, but I can’t do that well unless I understand it concretely. Can you give me 2-3 specific examples of where my work or behavior missed the mark, what impact you saw, and what you want done differently?”

If you want the more honest version:

“I’m concerned that the feedback was too vague to be actionable. I need […] examples and clear expectations so I can respond fairly and effectively.”

What each costs:
- The first version is more repairable. It preserves the relationship and makes them do the work of being specific.
- The second version is more […]. It may feel better, but it raises the temperature and makes escalation more likely.

If you do go over their head, keep it factual, not psychological:

“I’ve received negative feedback that I’m trying to act on, but I haven’t been able to get […] examples or clear expectations despite asking. I’d like help aligning on what success looks like.”

Do not say, “My boss is threatened by me.” That asks the next person to buy into your motive story instead of the facts.

A practical decision rule:
- Stay with your boss if they can name specifics and a path forward.
- Escalate if they cannot or will not give specifics after you ask clearly, or if there’s evidence the vagueness is being used against you.
