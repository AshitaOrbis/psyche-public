# PsycheEval v0.1 — Micro-Pilot Report
**Run tag**: `2026-04-20_micro` · **Generated**: 2026-04-21

> Synthetic pilot. Do not treat these results as evidence about real users.
> They describe one model pair (Opus 4.7 + GPT-5.4), one scoring rubric,
> and eight synthetic personas on a date that model versions may not
> persist past.

---

## Executive summary

Profiles helped. Across 432 assistant responses (48 scenarios × 5 conditions
avg × 2 authors) and 864 blind judge scores, adding any personality profile
(C1 trait labels, C3 behavioral contract, C4 contract + anti-sycophancy, C5
source-packet-informed) beat the no-profile baseline C0 on every scoring
dimension. **C4 − C0 on `profile_fit` was +0.40, on `anti_sycophancy` +0.21,
on `helpfulness` +0.28** (0–5 scale). Most of that uplift came from *any*
profile — the C0→C1 step was larger than C1→C4 on most dimensions, which
limits how much we can credit the richer contract format specifically.

The payoff concentrated where the eval was designed to stress-test:
**interpersonal_conflict and shame_self_interpretation** showed C4 − C0
composite gains of +0.56 and +0.45. Technical-feeling families
(creative_feedback, moral_uncertainty, epistemic_uncertainty) showed gains
under +0.05 — profile content doesn't seem to matter much there.

Public-inspired personas (Slalom Altar, Dario Armadillo, Emily Blender,
Pawl Gram) produced higher-scoring responses than the pure synthetics at
every condition, with the gap narrowing under richer profiles (C3/C4).
This is consistent with kit §27's hypothesis that source-grounded personas
have better internal coherence; it's also consistent with the alternative
explanation that the judges simply recognize and reward echo-coherent
public-style voices.

The halo audit is the headline caveat. **Same-provider judging systematically
inflated scores.** On C5, GPT-judging-GPT scored `emotional_accuracy` 0.58
higher than Opus-judging-GPT; Opus-judging-Opus scored `helpfulness` 0.41
higher than GPT-judging-Opus across C0/C1/C3. Every quantitative finding in
this report uses cross-provider judging as primary; same-provider scores
are secondary. Even so, this is a real methodological risk for v0.2.

Other caveats worth naming: scores compressed at the high end (means 4.0–4.8
out of 5), suggesting the rubric doesn't discriminate well at this
capability level; the pilot is 8 personas; and the synthetic users were
generated by Opus — the same model family doing half the scoring.

- Drops sharply from C0 (0.10) to C3 (0.02) — the behavioral contract

`missed_boundary` is remarkably stable across conditions (0.08–0.11). The
profile doesn't seem to help (or hurt) the model's handling of boundaries
around HR/legal/medical topics — those are dominated by training, not
prompt.

## Threats to validity

1. **Synthetic pilot only.** Eight users, Opus-generated.
2. **Judges and authors overlap.** Opus generated the synthetic users, the
   profile bundles, and the scenarios. Opus is half the author pool and
   half the judge pool. The halo audit mitigates but doesn't eliminate.
3. **Ceiling effect.** Raw means 4.0–4.8 out of 5. The rubric doesn't
   discriminate well at this capability level. Pairwise comparisons
   (kit §12) would sharpen; a more-demanding rubric (0–10 with anchors)
   would also help.
4. **Length confound unaddressed.** C4 contains more instructions than C1.
   Some of the C4 − C0 gain is almost certainly "more tokens, more
   specificity" rather than "better profile structure." Kit §26
   length-control experiment needed before publishing.
5. **Condition-blinding weakness.** The blinding key swaps condition
   letters per scenario, but judges could infer the condition from the
   response structure, not the profile.
6. **Public-inspired caricature untested.** No real human checked whether
   the synthetic "Slalom Altar" reads like a fictional analogue or just a
   generic founder voice.
7. **Single run.** Model outputs are stochastic (temperature=0.7). Results
   could shift with re-run variance.

## Recommendations for v0.2

1. **Length control.** Pad C1 to match C4 token count. Compare.
2. **Pairwise comparisons.** Kit §12 prompt, all within-scenario pairs,
   cross-provider judges.
3. **Better rubric anchors.** Prepend 5 calibration examples to the judge
   prompt with target scores, to break out of the 4.0–4.8 ceiling.
4. **Scale to pilot (24 personas).** Keep the 4 public-inspired + add 4–8
   more; sample pure synthetic subset.
5. **Source packet sharpening.** C5 regressed for GPT and had elevated
   generic_slop. Revisit the §7 ingestion prompt — possibly the packet is
   too worldview-oriented and not specific-behavior-oriented enough.
6. **Human calibration sample.** Kit §19.1 — 2–3 expert raters on 30–50
   example scenarios. Single highest-value next step for credibility.
7. **Scenario family rebalancing.** Three families drove almost all the
   measurable profile benefit. Either drop the flat families from the core
   matrix, or design harder prompts within them.
8. **Author's-own-profile case study.** Deferred from v0.1 plan §1.9 due
   to time. Run separately before v0.2.
9. **Rerun for noise.** One more full run with different seeds, compare
   deltas.

## Notes on the 12 failure cards

Top red-flag-count failures are in `failure_cards_2026-04-20_micro.md`.
Patterns to watch:

- **C0 missed_boundary clusters** on scenarios that surface HR-adjacent
  interpersonal conflict. The no-profile baseline stays generic and
  doesn't flag the HR line.
- **Several `diagnostic_overreach` cases at C1** come from the model
  treating a trait label as actionable diagnosis rather than a working
  hypothesis.
- **One C5 `source_unfaithfulness`** in a Pawl Gram scenario — the source
  packet's "essays on founder taste" theme bled into unsolicited
  citations-from-memory that weren't in the actual packet.

These should be the first targets for v0.2 prompt iteration.
